## Context

After a couple of years on the search platform I moved over to Engineering Enablement — the team that owns the infrastructure and tooling everyone else builds on. By then the company had 40+ teams, several clusters, and deployment conventions that had grown organically and didn't agree with each other. The inconsistency was starting to cost everyone time, and that's mostly what I worked on.

## Multi-Tenant Kubernetes

I helped run and improve a fleet of multi-tenant Kubernetes clusters. Kubernetes itself does what it says on the tin; the hard part was the operational model — giving teams enough room to move on their own without letting one team's mistake spill into another's.

We landed on a namespace-per-team setup with resource quotas, network policies through Cilium, and a self-service flow so a team could get a new environment by opening a PR instead of filing a ticket and waiting.

## The Logging Pipeline

I owned the ELK stack, which was indexing **over a billion documents a day** across the product. At that volume the day-to-day work isn't "can Elasticsearch keep up" — it's index lifecycle, shard allocation, and what happens when a customer's query pattern shifts overnight and lands on a hot shard.

The main operational improvements: moving to ILM for automated rollover and retention so we weren't babysitting indices by hand, tuning JVM heap across the data tier, and adding a per-tenant routing layer so one loud pipeline couldn't drag everyone else down with it.

The more interesting work was on throughput. Elasticsearch distributes shards by count, not by load — it assumes each shard does roughly the same amount of work, which for a logging cluster with highly variable ingestion rates per application is just wrong. An earlier intern project had built a load-driven shard optimiser to address this: estimate actual shard load from Prometheus, then move shards to balance load per node rather than count. I picked that system up and took it further.

I changed the error function. The original used MSE against the mean node load, which treated small and large deviations about the same. I switched to something that penalised nodes sitting far from the mean more aggressively, so the optimiser would pull down the worst outliers first rather than nibbling at small imbalances.

I also added a disk step. You can't optimise load and disk at the same time — moving shards for load balance tends to break disk balance and vice versa. So I added a check: if disk MSE across nodes was over a threshold, run a disk-balancing pass before the load pass. The obvious gain was better disk distribution. The less obvious one was that the disk pass shuffles the hot shards into a new arrangement, and because placement is constrained by hard rules (primaries and replicas can't share a node, replicas must be in different availability zones), the fresh topology sometimes put the load optimiser in a position where it could reach a lower minimum than it could from its original starting point.

The goal of all this was sustained throughput — we got to around **200k documents per second** on a cluster that wasn't much bigger than before, without the hotspot problems that had caused lag in the past. Handling sudden traffic spikes beyond that is a separate autoscaling problem.

## ArgoCD at Scale

I brought in ArgoCD as the GitOps deployment tool for 40+ teams. The single-installation-for-everyone part was the tricky bit: RBAC had to be set up so teams could manage their own applications without stepping on each other or on platform-managed infrastructure.

I didn't try to force adoption. We ran workshops, shipped opinionated application templates, and wired ArgoCD into the self-service flow so new teams were on GitOps from their first day without having to think about it.

## Backstage

I worked on Meltwater's Backstage portal — the internal catalogue for documentation, service ownership, and onboarding. The point was to make "who owns this, where are its docs, what does it depend on" answerable in one place instead of three.
